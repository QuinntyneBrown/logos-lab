Abena Mensah is a theologian who writes about what it means to be human. Dr. Mensah's work in theological anthropology asks how the church's teaching that we are made in the image of God should shape the way we build and use machines that speak and reason.

Dr. Mensah teaches systematic theology, leads the Imago Dei cohort on Logos Lab each autumn, and preaches regularly in congregations across the Maritimes and Ontario. The book Made, Not Manufactured came out in 2025. Read the Faculty page (external site, opens in a new tab) or the expert directory.

Default variant: the expert bio from html, with an external link carrying externalLabel. AC-1, AC-9, AC-10

Augustine never saw a computer. But few writers have thought harder about what it is to remember, to pay attention and to be distracted, and those are exactly the things large language models seem to do.

What a model “remembers”

A language model is trained on a vast body of text and learns which words tend to follow which. When it answers, it is not recalling an experience. It is predicting, very well, what a plausible answer would look like.1

That difference matters. The psalmist asks, Psalm 8:4 (NRSV), “what are human beings that you are mindful of them?” God's remembering is personal. A model's “memory” is statistical. Paul closes the argument at 1 Corinthians 15:51–57 (NRSV).

“A store of data is not a self. Augustine would have said that a self is a memory turned toward love.”

Attention, then and now

The garden in Milan where he heard a child's voice say tolle, lege, “take up and read”, is a story about attention redirected. Our tools are built to hold attention.

Three questions to take with you

  • What do I hope a chatbot will remember for me, and what should I remember myself?
  • Where does my attention go when I reach for a screen?
  • How would I describe the difference between being known and being predicted?
  1. Dr. Joel Santos explains this well in Inside a Neural Network in Ten Minutes. ↩
