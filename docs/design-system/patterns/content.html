Content and tone

Write the way a thoughtful colleague talks: calm, warm, specific and plain.

Overview

The words carry the look as much as the colours do. Logos Lab sounds like a thoughtful colleague sitting across a wooden table: calm, warm and unhurried, never loud, never cute. It says exactly what is happening, in plain words, and then gets out of the way.

When to use

These rules apply to every word in the product: page titles, buttons, labels, help, errors, empty states, notifications, emails and documentation. When a team adapts the kit, they rewrite the sample copy in their own domain but keep this voice. Legal text keeps its meaning, but still uses plain words where it can.

Structure

Most messages have the same three parts, in this order. Leave out any part that adds nothing.

  1. What is true — the state or outcome, naming the object: "Field Day wasn't published."
  2. What it means — one short sentence of context or reassurance: "Nothing was sent to the team."
  3. What to do — a button or link that starts with a verb: Try again.

Field Day wasn't published

Nothing was sent to the team, and your details are still here.

States

Each state has its own job, so each has its own kind of sentence.

  • Empty: what will be here and how to start. "No projects yet. Start one and invite whoever you work with."
  • Loading: present tense with an ellipsis. "Saving…", "Importing 42 of 120 pages…"
  • Success: past tense, naming the object. "Invitation sent to Grace Liu."
  • Error: what didn't happen, reassurance, then the fix. Never blame the person.
  • Destructive: name exactly what will be lost and whether it can be undone.
  • Offline or degraded: what still works. "You can keep writing; changes save when you reconnect."

Importing 42 of 120 pages…

Invitation sent to Grace Liu

Couldn't save. Your note is still here.

No gatherings planned

Plan one and everyone on Northwind gets an invitation.

Responsive behaviour

Write so that the words survive any width.

  • Put the important word first, so a wrapped line still makes sense: "Field Day · Sat 17 Oct, 10:00 am".
  • Never rely on truncation to shorten copy; write it shorter instead. Names and titles always wrap.
  • Leave room for longer languages: German and French run about a third longer than English. Don't size buttons or columns to the English text.
  • On small screens, keep the same words; don't swap in abbreviations a person has to decode.

Accessibility

Visible text is the name. A button's accessible name starts with its visible label, so voice control works ("Archive", not an aria-label of "Remove item").

Links make sense out of context. Screen reader users often list links; "See how projects work" works there, "Learn more" doesn't.

Say it in words. Status, success and error are always stated, never only shown by colour or an icon.

Machine-readable dates. Wrap dates and times in <time datetime="2026-10-17T10:00"> so assistive tech and calendars can read them.

Plain language helps everyone, including people reading in a second language and people with cognitive disabilities. Aim for sentences under 20 words.

Field Day is on Sat 17 Oct, 10:00 am. See who's coming to Field Day

Inclusive and local

  • Use names people choose; ask for "Full name", not "First name" and "Last name".
  • Use "they" when a person's pronouns aren't known.
  • Avoid idioms, metaphors about ability ("blind spot", "crippled"), and jokes that don't translate.
  • Use the reader's local format for dates, times, numbers and distances; this page shows the default.
  • Describe images for what they show, in a short phrase, in their alt text.
