Inside a Neural Network in Ten Minutes

Dr. Joel Santos · Published 4 Sep 2026

0:00 / 10:12

About this video

A plain-language tour of what happens inside a neural network, with no maths beyond addition. Dr. Joel Santos builds the smallest useful network on a whiteboard and shows how training changes it.

This video is a free preview: anyone can watch it without an account.

Dr. Joel Santos is a machine-learning researcher who has spent the last decade training language and vision models, and the last three years explaining them to seminarians, pastors and parish councils. In this video he works through a single, complete example: a network that learns to tell a Psalm from a Proverb using nothing but word counts. Along the way he names each moving part (inputs, weights, activations, loss and gradient descent) and shows what changes, and what does not, when the same idea is scaled up to the systems people now talk to every day.

Transcript

Select a time to play from there.

  1. A neural network is a very long sum with adjustable numbers in it.

  2. Training means nudging those numbers until the answers improve.

  3. Each number is called a weight. A large model has billions of them.

  4. None of the weights means anything on its own. Meaning, if there is any, lives in the pattern.

  5. Let me show you the smallest network that still does something useful.

  6. Here are two inputs: how often a passage says “Lord”, and how often it says “wise” or “wisdom”, counted across the whole of the Psalms and the whole of Proverbs.

  7. Each input is multiplied by a weight, the results are added together, and the total is squashed into a number between zero and one, which we read as the network's confidence.

  8. At the start the weights are random, so the network is guessing. Training is the patient, entirely mechanical work of making those guesses a little less wrong each time.
